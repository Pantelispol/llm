## History

Standing prominently on the waterfront, the White Tower is Thessaloniki’s most recognizable civic landmark. Erected by the Ottomans in the late fifteenth to early sixteenth century (c. 1450–1535 CE) following the conquest of the city in 1430, the circular tower replaced an earlier twelfth-century Byzantine seawall fortification. Originally integrated into the maritime defensive ramparts (which were largely demolished in 1866–1867), it served successively as a garrison, fortress, and a notorious Ottoman prison known as the "Tower of Blood." It acquired its present name after being whitewashed in the late nineteenth century (c. 1890), and today it anchors the modern seafront promenade.

## What to see

Inside, a compact museum presentation introduces Thessaloniki through successive historical periods. The route rises through the circular structure, allowing visitors to notice the thick masonry, narrow passages, and organization of the tower as well as the exhibition. The upper level gives broad views over the Thermaic Gulf, the waterfront promenade, and the dense city center.

## Practical tips

The visit involves an upward interior route and repeated stairs, so travelers with mobility concerns should verify the current accessible route before relying on the tower as a viewpoint. The roof and the surrounding promenade can be exposed to sun, wind, and rain. The tower sits beside the waterfront and combines naturally with nearby museums, the Umbrellas sculpture, or a walk along Nea Paralia.
