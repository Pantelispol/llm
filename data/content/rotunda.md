## History

The Rotunda was constructed around 306 CE (early fourth century) as part of the imperial palace complex of the Roman emperor Galerius. Converted into a Christian church in the late fourth century (c. 390–400 CE), the structure was adorned with renowned early Christian wall mosaics. In 1590, during the Ottoman period, it was transformed into a mosque (Hortaci Efendi Mosque), retaining its minaret today. It is included among Thessaloniki’s UNESCO-listed Paleochristian and Byzantine monuments, reflecting over sixteen centuries of architectural and religious evolution.

## What to see

The immense circular interior is the main experience. A high brick dome creates an unusually unified space, while surviving early Christian mosaics retain figures, architectural settings, and rich decorative surfaces. Changes made during different periods are visible alongside the Roman fabric. Outside, the cylindrical mass and nearby minaret make the building’s layered history easy to read from the surrounding streets.

## Practical tips

The Rotunda is also associated with Christian worship, so visitors should dress modestly, speak quietly, and avoid disrupting services. Interior surfaces and mosaic details reward slow looking, while the nearby Arch of Galerius provides immediate context for the wider Roman complex. The surrounding university quarter is busy and largely walkable, though paving and the monument entrance should be checked for individual accessibility needs.
